1. Meaning, Nature and Purpose of Summary Judgment

Summary Judgment • Ogun State5
Judicial Webinar06 / 42

Summary judgment has been defined in a plethora of cases as a judgment given in favour of a Claimant without a plenary or full trial of the action. Although not preceded by a trial, a summary judgment is a final judgment on the merits. As explained by Felix Nwadialo in Civil Procedure in Nigeria, this is because a summary judgment is based on the baseline want of a defence by the Defendant, and a subsequent full trial of the action cannot alter this objective situation. A summary judgment, therefore, unlike a default judgment, cannot be whimsically set aside by the court that granted it; it can generally only be upset on appeal. This principle was clearly re-echoed by the Court of Appeal in Nasco Town Plc v. Mr. Festus Ude Nwabueze (2014) LPELR-22526(CA).

Summary Judgment • Ogun State6
Judicial Webinar07 / 42

Summary judgment procedures originated in 19th-century English common law to stop Defendants from deploying deceitful or delayed tactics—traditionally termed "sham pleadings"—in commercial debt cases. Over time, it evolved into a foundational pre-trial tool across Britain, the United States, and other common law jurisdictions, including Nigeria. The essence of this summary procedure is to enable a Claimant whose case is unassailable to obtain a quick dispatch of the suit, especially where it involves specific debts or liquidated money claims.

1.1 The Evolution of the Summary Trial

Summary Judgment • Ogun State7
Judicial Webinar08 / 42

Traditionally, the mode of trial in common law courts was strictly characterized by oral testimony in open court, matching examination-in-chief with rigorous cross-examination. Summary judgment procedure, by contrast, is deployed in cases where the Claimant believes the Defendant has no defence to the action, or when the claim is a liquidated money demand or simple debt recovery. It is guaranteed to save time and costs, accelerate the process of debt recovery, and avoid the hassles of an elongated trial. It is granted where the court finds that the Defendant has no defence to the claim. In some instances, summary determination may occur even before extensive pleadings are exchanged—such as under the traditional "Undefended List" procedure. See the case of UBA Plc v. Jargaba (2007) LPELR-3399(SC).

Summary Judgment • Ogun State8
Judicial Webinar09 / 42

Before the advent of civil summary trial procedures, the legal system possessed no filter to weed out meritless or uncontested cases before trial. At common law, a Claimant was completely at the mercy of a Defendant’s willingness to prolong litigation. The following restrictive practices strictly obtained in civil litigation prior to these reforms:

Mandatory Full Trials for Every Dispute: Even if a Defendant openly admitted to a debt in private or had absolutely no legal or factual defence, the court could not grant judgment on the merits without a full trial. Claimants were legally compelled to go through every formal stage of litigation: issuing a writs or plaints, exchanging extensive written pleadings, conducting pre-trial discovery, and presenting oral arguments.

Summary Judgment • Ogun State9
Judicial Webinar10 / 42

Exploitation of "Sham Pleadings" by Debtors: Before statutory summary procedures were introduced—such as, Order 11 of the High Court of Ogun State Civil Procedure Rules, or Orders 6 and 9 of the Magistrates' Courts Civil Procedure Rules of Ogun State—the most significant defect in civil procedure was the court's inability to test the factual truth of a defence before the trial itself. Debtors routinely used sham or fictitious pleadings, inventing fake disputes or relying on hyper-technical pleading errors, solely to buy time, frustrate creditors, and deplete the plaintiff's financial resources.

Summary Judgment • Ogun State10
Judicial Webinar11 / 42

Severe Stagnation of Commerce: Because collecting an unpaid debt or enforcing a simple contract required years of formal litigation, the merchant class faced immense financial risks. Dishonorable businesses could default on bills of exchange or promissory notes with practical impunity, knowing the courts were choked by the procedural requirement to treat a complex land title dispute and a simple unpaid commercial debt with the exact same procedural rigidity.

The summary judgment procedure was thus invented to speed up the legal process, reduce court backlogs, and save time and money by resolving straightforward civil disputes without the long delays and complex formalities of a traditional trial.

1.2 The Nature of Summary Judgment

Summary Judgment • Ogun State11
Judicial Webinar12 / 42

Summary judgment is not simply an abbreviated version of an ordinary trial. It is a distinct procedural mechanism whose availability, requirements, and consequences depend strictly on the applicable law and rules of court. Unlike an ordinary trial, where disputed facts are tested through oral testimony, cross-examination, and the evaluation of physical evidence, summary judgment ordinarily involves a rigorous assessment of the processes and materials prescribed by the governing rules. These include affidavits, pleadings, front-loaded documentary exhibits, and written addresses.

Summary Judgment • Ogun State12
Judicial Webinar13 / 42

The court's task is not to conduct a full trial on paper. Rather, it must determine whether the materials placed before it discloses a genuine, triable issue that requires resolution through the ordinary trial process, or whether the applicable legal threshold for summary judgment has been satisfied. This distinction is critical: a Magistrate must resist the temptation to resolve substantial conflicts of fact through an abbreviated procedure when the rules and the circumstances of the case require a trial. Equally, the mere assertion of a defence does not establish that a genuine dispute exists. The substance of the defence, rather than the mere fact that one has been filed, must be assessed in accordance with the governing procedure.

1.3 The Purpose of Summary Judgment

The procedure serves several imperative purposes in the administration of civil justice:

Summary Judgment • Ogun State13
Judicial Webinar14 / 42

Clearing the Cause List: Traditional trials take a massive amount of time, personnel, and institutional resources. Summary trials let judges and magistrates handle clear-cut cases quickly so the court system does not collapse under heavy caseloads.

Cutting Formalities: By skipping full witness depositions, extensive pre-trial discovery, or prolonged trial stages, the proceeding moves fast (brevi manu, or "by quick hand").

Bridging Settlement Gaps: In civil cases, the summary judgment process gives the opposing party a quick preview of how the bench perceives the strengths of the case, helping them settle out of court rather than going through the full rigours of a trial.

Promoting Expeditious Determination: This aligns with the constitutional aspiration that civil rights and obligations should be determined fairly and within a reasonable time.

Summary Judgment • Ogun State14
Judicial Webinar15 / 42

Protecting Litigants from Unnecessary Expense: Litigation involves legal fees, transportation costs, repeated attendance in court, and considerable personal or commercial inconvenience. Compelling parties to undergo a trial where it is unnecessary imposes a disproportionate burden.

Discouraging the Misuse of Court Processes as an Instrument of Delay: A Defendant should not be permitted to prolong a claim indefinitely through a defence that, upon proper examination, discloses no genuine answer to the Claimant's case.

These objectives, however, must never be pursued at the expense of procedural justice. Efficiency is a vital attribute of justice, but it cannot independently justify the premature determination of a genuinely contested claim.

2. Distinguishing Summary Judgment from Parallel Procedures

As Magistrates, an important preliminary consideration is the need to distinguish summary judgment from other distinct procedures that may also result in the determination of a case without a full trial.

Summary Judgment • Ogun State15
Judicial Webinar16 / 42

Default judgment: Default judgment ordinarily arises where a party fails to take a step required by the applicable Rules—for example, failing to appear or to file a defence and the conditions prescribed for judgment in default have been satisfied. The court must verify the relevant default, compliance with service and notice requirements, and the legal availability of the relief sought. A Defendant’s silence is not the same as an express admission of liability.

Judgment on admission: Judgment on admission rests on an admission sufficiently clear to justify judgment in respect of the admitted matter, under the applicable law or Rules. The court must identify what has actually been admitted and must not enlarge an admission beyond its proper scope. An admission that a loan was obtained, for example, is not necessarily an admission that the entire original amount remains unpaid.

Summary Judgment • Ogun State16
Judicial Webinar17 / 42

Consent judgment: A consent judgment gives judicial effect to terms freely agreed by the parties and accepted by the court in accordance with the applicable procedure. The court should satisfy itself that the terms are clear, lawful and genuinely agreed. A consent judgment is not a determination of a contested claim after a trial; it reflects the parties’ settlement as recognized by the court.

Undefended-list procedure: An undefended-list procedure, where applicable under the governing Rules, is a distinct statutory or procedural mechanism with its own scope and requirements. It must not be assumed to exist in a court merely because another court’s Rules contain such a procedure.

Small Claims procedure: A Small Claims procedure is another distinct regime intended to facilitate the resolution of qualifying claims through simplified steps and prescribed timelines. Its jurisdictional limits, forms, timelines and territorial operation must be taken from the current governing instruments.

Summary Judgment • Ogun State17
Judicial Webinar18 / 42

Although these procedures may share the objective of avoiding unnecessary trials, they are not interchangeable. Each has its own legal foundation, conditions and procedural safeguards. A court must therefore first of all identify the procedure properly invoked and apply its requirements rather than import the conditions of one procedure into another. This distinction is particularly significant in the Magistrates' Courts of Ogun State.

3. The Legal Framework Governing Summary Judgments in the Ogun State Magistrate Courts.

Before determining whether a claim can be determined without a full trial, the Magistrate should ask: What law and procedural rules governs this Court and this proceeding?

Summary Judgment • Ogun State18
Judicial Webinar19 / 42

A Magistrate must first identify the applicable legislation and rules of court before considering whether a claim qualifies for summary determination. The existence of a procedure under the High Court Rules does not, without an applicable statutory or procedural basis, automatically establish that the same procedure is available in the Magistrates’ Courts. This preliminary enquiry is not a mere technicality as the authority of a court to adopt a particular procedure, the conditions governing its exercise, and the safeguards available to the parties must be derived from relevant laws.

Summary Judgment • Ogun State19
Judicial Webinar20 / 42

Summary proceedings at the magisterial level derive authority from the Ogun State Magistrates’ Courts Law, the associated Magistrates' Courts (Civil Procedure) Rules, and the operational guidelines of the Ogun State Small Claims Court. The provisions of the Ogun State High Court Civil Procedure Rules offer useful comparative insight, but they should not be treated as automatically governing proceedings before a Magistrate unless stipulated by the applicable Rules which is not the case in Ogun State presently.

3.1 Natively Codified Summary Provisions

The closest native provisions within the Magistrates' Courts (Civil Procedure) Rules, Laws of Ogun State 2006, focus primarily on admissions, agreements(consent) and defaults:

Summary Judgment • Ogun State20
Judicial Webinar21 / 42

Order 6, Rule 1 provides for admission of Debts or Part of Debts as follow: Any person served with a plaint may file a signed written statement admitting the claim in whole or in part. The Registrar must notify the plaintiff, and it becomes unnecessary for the plaintiff to prove the admitted claim. The court, at its next sitting, enters judgment based on the admission.

Order 6, Rule 2 provides for agreement as to Amount and Conditions of Payment): If the parties agree upon the amount and terms of payment, they may sign a statement in the presence of the Registrar. The court shall enter judgment on those agreed terms, which has the same enforceable effect as a judgment on the merits.

Order 9, Rule 6(2): If the Plaintiff fails to appear but the Defendant appears and admits the claim to the full amount, the Magistrate may proceed to give judgment as if the Plaimant had appeared.

Order 9, Rule 9: Provides for default judgment where the Defendant completely fails to appear.

What the above provisions indicate is that the native 2006 Magistrates’ Rules in Ogun State primarily accommodate summary outcomes via admissions, consent, and defaults. They lack a standalone, dedicated "Summary Judgment" order like the High Court Rules do (e.g., Order 11 of the High Court Rules). However, since the focus of this paper is Summary Judgements in the Magistrates Courts of Ogun State, we shall restrict ourselves to the procedures provided under the enabling Law/Rules guiding the said Court.

3.3 The Three Procedural Pathways for Summary Resolution

A practitioner seeking summary resolution in an Ogun State Magistrate Court must navigate the system using one of three distinct procedural pathways:

Pathway A: The Liquidated Money Pathway (Admission of Claim)

If the action is strictly for a liquidated money demand and the Defendant appears and cannot dispute the debt or explicitly admits the claim in their processes, the Magistrate skips a full trial entirely and enters judgment based on the admission.

Summary Judgment • Ogun State22
Judicial Webinar23 / 42

Pathway B: The Consent Judgement Pathway (Terms of Settlement)

Sometimes, after the plaint is served on the Defendant, parties agree to settle by filing Terms of settlement. Subsequently, the Magistrate can skip full trial and enter judgement based on the agreement of parties. Thus, where parties to a dispute, freely enter into terms of settlement which is then made the Judgement of the Court, this is called a Consent Judgement, and it is no longer open to the court to adjudicate on the subject matter of the dispute.

Pathway C: The Modern Alternative (Small Claims Court Procedure)

The Small Claims Court is operationalized across many Magisterial Districts in Ogun State (e.g., Abeokuta, Ota, Ijebu-Ode, Sagamu, Idi-Oke and others).

Key points

Jurisdictional Limit: It is strictly limited to liquidated monetary claims or simple debt disputes not exceeding ₦1,000,000 (excluding interest).

Summary Judgment • Ogun State23
Judicial Webinar24 / 42

The Fast-Track System: This is a native summary framework designed to bypass complex legal maneuvering. The Claimant files a Letter of Demand alongside Form SCA2.

Strict Timelines: There are no lengthy pleadings. The entire procedure is heavily compressed—the hearing must happen within 30 days, and the entire proceeding from filing to judgment is strictly capped at 60 days.

3.4 Step-by-Step Procedure

Filing: The Claimant files the Plaint/Summons.

Service: The originating process and supporting documents must be personally served on the Defendant by the Court Bailiff (or via regularized substituted service).

Response Window: The Defendant must file a Defence, admission or counter claim within 7 days of receipt of the process (or Form SCA5 in Small Claims matters).

Effect of Inaction: If the Defendant does nothing after being duly served, the court will hear the case and enter judgment.

Summary Judgment • Ogun State24
Judicial Webinar25 / 42

Hearing and Determination: The case is fixed for hearing. If the Magistrate finds the Defendant truly has no defence, judgment is entered for the Claimant. If a good defence (a defence on the merits) is disclosed, the Magistrate will proceed to trial.

Preliminaries at the Small Claims Court: At the first appearance of the parties before the Court, the Magistrate is mandated to promote, encourage and facilitate amicable settlement. Parties are also encouraged to contact each. This must however take place within 7 days only. The case proceeds to trial and determination where settlement failed.

4. The Constitutional Guarantee of Fair Hearing

The constitutional foundation of this entire discussion is Section 36(1) of the Constitution of the Federal Republic of Nigeria 1999 (as amended). This provision guarantees every person the right to a fair hearing within a reasonable time by a court or tribunal established by law and constituted in a manner that secures its independence and impartiality.

Summary Judgment • Ogun State25
Judicial Webinar26 / 42

Two important considerations emerge from this provision.

First, the Constitution recognizes the importance of determining disputes within a reasonable time. Justice delayed may impose substantial hardship on litigants, undermine confidence in the courts and diminish the practical value of a remedy.

Second, the requirement of a fair hearing places a constitutional limit on the manner in which judicial proceedings may be conducted. Speed cannot justify a process that deprives a party of a fair opportunity to present or answer the case against that party.

These considerations are complementary rather than contradictory. The Constitution does not require every civil dispute to proceed through a full trial regardless of the applicable law or the issues involved. Equally, it does not permit a court to sacrifice a party’s right to fair hearing merely to achieve a quicker disposition.

The judicial responsibility is therefore to pursue expedition through lawful and fair procedures.

4.1 The Principles of Natural Justice

Summary Judgment • Ogun State26
Judicial Webinar27 / 42

The right to a fair hearing is anchored upon two pillars of natural justice:

• Audi alteram partem: (Hear the other side) and Nemo judex in causa sua: (No person should be a judge in their own cause). In the context of summary judgment, audi alteram partem is highly significant. Before a court enters judgment through a procedure that dispenses with a full trial, the party against whom judgment is sought must receive the real, meaningful opportunity prescribed by the rules to contest the application. The court must strictly verify:

Whether the relevant processes were properly and legally served.

Whether the party was afforded the statutory time required by law to respond.

Whether the response raises a genuine issue that requires a trial.

The obligation to hear a party does not mean the court must accept every frivolous defence or permit a claim to proceed to trial simply because the Defendant objects to judgment. Fair hearing guarantees a fair opportunity to participate; it does not guarantee a favourable outcome.

Summary Judgment • Ogun State27
Judicial Webinar28 / 42

Where the prescribed procedure has been followed, the parties have received their lawful opportunity to be heard, and the legal threshold has been met, a court may grant summary judgment without violating the Constitution.

4.2 Judicial Authorities on Summary Judgment

In United Bank for Africa Plc & Anor v. Jargaba (2007) 11 NWLR (Pt. 1045) 247, the Supreme Court explained that summary procedures are intended to dispose promptly of virtually uncontested cases and should not be used to prolong litigation where there is no reasonable basis for a defence. The law seeks to prevent unnecessary trials, not to prevent the trial of genuine disputes.

4.3 The Magistrate’s Duty to Apply the Correct Procedure

The Magistrate's first duty is to ascertain the legal basis for the procedure invoked.

The second is to ensure compliance with the prescribed requirements.

The third is to determine, on the materials properly before the court, whether the conditions for granting judgment have been met.

Summary Judgment • Ogun State28
Judicial Webinar29 / 42

These responsibilities require particular care because proceedings for Default judgment, Consent judgement and judgements on admissions must each be considered according to their governing provisions. We cannot elucidate on Summary Judgment Procedure as provided for under the High Court Civil Procedure Rules or the Undefended List Procedure as there is no provision for it under the Magistrate Court Civil Procedure Rules of Ogun State and there is no law which enables the Magistrate to import the provisions of the High Civil Procedure Rules.

The above becomes imperative because a decision reached through an unauthorised procedure, or through a procedure applied without observing its essential safeguards, may expose the proceedings to challenge on appeal. The guiding principle is straightforward: the power to determine a claim summarily must be exercised within the law, and the manner of exercising that power must remain consistent with the constitutional guarantee of fair hearing.

Balancing Expeditious Justice with the Right to Fair Hearing

Balancing expeditious justice with the right to a fair hearing is one of the most critical challenges facing modern legal systems. In Nigeria, this tension plays out directly between the systemic need to clear clogged cause lists vis a vis the unyielding protections of Section 36 of the 1999 Nigerian Constitution, which guarantees a fair hearing. The core of the issue lies in the fact that while "justice delayed is justice denied," it is equally true that "justice hurried is justice buried". If a court prioritizes speed so aggressively that it limits a party's ability to present their case, the entire legal proceeding becomes a nullity.

Summary Judgment • Ogun State30
Judicial Webinar31 / 42

The true test of judicial competence in the application of summary judgment lies in the ability to distinguish between a case that requires a trial and one that does not. The Magistrate must neither permit a litigant to frustrate the judicial process through a baseless defence nor allow the desire for speedy disposal to override the fundamental right to fair hearing. This balance is central to the administration of justice. A judgment delivered quickly but in disregard of the law or the rights of a party may generate further litigation, occasion injustice and undermine public confidence in the courts. Conversely, compelling parties to undergo a full trial where the applicable procedure permits a lawful summary determination may impose unnecessary expense and delay.

The objective, therefore, is not speed at all costs. It is the efficient administration of justice within the framework of the law. Balanced against speed is the sacrosanct doctrine of natural justice, codified under Section 36(1) of the 1999 Constitution i.e

Summary Judgment • Ogun State31
Judicial Webinar32 / 42

Audi alteram partem (hear both sides): The absolute right of every Defendant to present their case and defend their civil obligations.

Nemo judex in causa sua (no one should be a judge in their own case): The requirement for an unbiased, detached arbiter.

Constitutional Precedence: As reaffirmed in Madukolu v. Nkemdilim (1962) LPELR-24023(SC), a denial of fair hearing invalidates the proceedings ab initio, rendering the resulting decision a nullity regardless of the merits.

5.1 The Vulnerability of Rushing Due Process : Tension Between Speed and Fair Hearing

Summary Judgment • Ogun State32
Judicial Webinar33 / 42

The tension between speed and fair hearing manifests daily. Magistrates must remember that Service of Process is the bedrock of jurisdiction. Speed must never bypass actual or constructive notification. If an Affidavit of Service turns out to be defective or fabricated, entering judgment constitutes a fatal jurisdictional error that renders the entire proceeding a nullity. Magistrates must meticulously inspect the Bailiff’s certificate of service before entertaining summary applications.

However, a fair hearing is an opportunity, not an absolute guarantee of success. If a Defendant is duly served and given the prescribed statutory time to reply but chooses to sleep on his rights, he cannot subsequently plead a denial of fair hearing. As held by the apex court in Newswatch Communications Ltd v. Atta (2006) LPELR-1986(SC), the right to a fair hearing cannot be used as an umbrella to protect indolent litigants.

Summary Judgment • Ogun State33
Judicial Webinar34 / 42

The Supreme Court maintains a zero-tolerance policy for shortcutting due process. In Chief Egbuchu v. Continental Merchant Bank Plc & Ors (2016) LPELR-40053(SC), the Court reiterated the settled principle that proceedings conducted in breach of the right to a fair hearing are liable to be set aside as a total nullity. Rushing a trial improperly will only result in the matter being sent back to be heard de novo (fresh), thereby wasting substantially more time and public resources.

5.2: Practical Courtroom Illustrations

Illustration 1

Consider a Claimant who commences an action to recover a debt of ₦5 million, producing a written loan agreement. The Defendant admits entering the agreement but asserts that ₦3 million has already been paid, providing specific evidence of bank transfers and receipts.

Question: What will you do as a Magistrate?

The Magistrate should not simply enter judgment for the entire ₦5 million because the contract is admitted. The alleged payments raise a material, triable issue concerning the outstanding balance.

Summary Judgment • Ogun State34
Judicial Webinar35 / 42

Suggested approach: The Magistrate should distinguish an admission that the loan was obtained from an admission that the entire sum remains outstanding. The alleged part-payment raises a material question concerning the amount due. The court should examine the processes and evidence under the applicable Rules and determine whether judgment on any expressly admitted amount is legally available. It should not grant partial summary judgment merely because the balance appears arithmetically ascertainable. Any disputed liability must be resolved through a procedure authorised by law.

Teaching point: An admission must be confined to its true scope. The court must not convert an admission of one fact into an admission of the whole claim, and it must not assume that partial summary judgment is available without identifying a legal basis.

Illustration III

During proceedings, both parties informed the court that they have resolved their dispute. They present signed terms of settlement asking that the terms be made the judgment of the court.

Question: What should the Magistrate consider?

Suggested approach: The Magistrate should confirm that the parties have genuinely agreed to the terms, that the terms are sufficiently clear and lawful, and that the court can give effect to them under the applicable Rules. If those requirements are met, the court may proceed under the relevant consent-judgment procedure. The court should not describe the outcome as summary judgment: its legal basis is the parties’ agreement and the rule governing consent judgments.

Teaching point: A judgment may be entered without a full trial for different reasons. Correct classification matters because the legal foundation and safeguards differ.

The above illustrations demonstrate that the quest for expeditious justice must remain anchored in the applicable procedural law. Since the Ogun State Magistrates’ Courts Civil Procedure Rules provide for judgment in default, judgment on admission and judgment by consent, those procedures must be distinguished and applied according to their respective requirements. They should not be indiscriminately treated as substitutes for a standalone summary judgment procedure.

Default should not be treated as an automatic entitlement to every relief claimed. The court must still act within its jurisdiction and the applicable Rules. The court should therefore proceed under the appropriate default-judgment provisions rather than treating the Defendant’s silence as a basis for importing an unprovided-for summary judgment procedure.

6. The Magistrate's Operational Checklist

Before deciding that a case does not need to go through the rigours of full trial, every Magistrate may use the following judicial checklist to ensure compliance with the law:

Summary Judgment • Ogun State38
Judicial Webinar39 / 42

Jurisdiction: Does the court have subject-matter and monetary jurisdiction over the claim?

Legal authority: Which specific provision of the applicable law or Rules authorises the procedure being invoked?

Proper classification: Is the matter one for default judgment, judgment on admission, consent judgment or another procedure expressly authorised by law or not authourised by law?

Service and notice: Have the prescribed requirements for service and notice been satisfied?

Procedural compliance: Have the conditions for invoking the particular procedure been met?

Scope of admission or default: What has actually been admitted, and what consequences does the applicable Rule attach to the default?

Fair hearing: Has each party received the opportunity required by law to be heard?

Relief: Is the relief sought legally available on the material properly before the court?

Reasons: Can the court explain the legal basis for its decision and demonstrate compliance with the applicable safeguards?

CONCLUSION: Speed Without Sacrificing Justice

Summary Judgment • Ogun State39
Judicial Webinar40 / 42

The analysis in this paper demonstrates that the proper application of summary judgment requires more than a mere desire to reduce the number of pending cases. It requires a clear understanding of the governing procedural rules, a careful assessment of the processes, and an unwavering commitment to the constitutional right to a fair hearing.

For Magistrates, the challenge is to ensure that cases which do not genuinely require a trial are determined without unnecessary delay, while disputes that require judicial investigation are allowed to proceed through the appropriate trial process. This calls for discernment, procedural discipline, and reasoned decision-making.

Summary Judgment • Ogun State40
Judicial Webinar41 / 42

A Magistrate must be firm against attempts to misuse the judicial process, yet cautious against prematurely terminating a genuine defence. The court must recognise that a weak defence is not invariably a sham, that documentary evidence does not automatically eliminate every triable issue, and that the right to a fair hearing is not an obstacle to efficiency but the very condition that makes adjudication legitimate.

The ultimate measure of judicial performance is not simply the speed with which cases are concluded. It is whether they are determined lawfully, fairly, and in a manner that commands public confidence in the administration of justice.

Permit me, therefore, to conclude this presentation with a question for us all to take back to our respective chambers: When a litigant leaves our courtroom, should that litigant remember only how quickly the case ended, or should they also be able to say, “I was heard, the law was applied, and justice was done”?

Our sacred duty is to ensure that both objectives are achieved.

Let us strive for courts that are:

Swift without being hasty.

Firm without being arbitrary and Efficient without sacrificing fairness.

For in the final analysis, the true achievement of justice is not merely that a case has ended or ended quickly but that it has ended through a process that respects the law, protects the rights of the parties and preserves public confidence in the judicial institution.
